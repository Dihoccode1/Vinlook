## POI-006 — Bảo tàng Chứng tích Chiến tranh

English name: **War Remnants Museum**.

### Narration EN

At the War Remnants Museum, photographs, documents and objects bring the human consequences of conflict into focus. Its predecessor opened on the fourth of September, nineteen seventy-five. The institution adopted its present name in nineteen ninety-five.

The museum describes its work as collecting and preserving evidence of war in Vietnam, while encouraging peace and friendship between peoples. That stated purpose provides context for the exhibitions. The materials are presented from the museum's perspective, and the stories ask visitors to consider both suffering and responsibility.

The collections include military equipment alongside more personal traces: wartime letters and badges worn in opposition to war. A large object can suggest the scale of a conflict. A letter or a small possession can bring attention back to an individual.

One example is the naval hat of Susan Schnall, a former United States Navy nurse. In the museum's account, she carried it while taking part in anti-war activities between nineteen sixty-eight and nineteen seventy-one. She donated the hat to the museum in May, twenty eighteen. Through this object, the museum connects a military uniform with a person's later work for peace.

Take time to read the context attached to each photograph and object. Consider who made it, who used it, and whose account accompanies it. These questions offer a thoughtful way to approach a place devoted to remembering war and considering what peace requires.

## POI-007 — Bảo tàng Lịch sử Việt Nam — Bảo tàng Lịch sử TP.HCM

English name: **Ho Chi Minh City History Museum**.

### Narration EN

Ho Chi Minh City History Museum brings together two kinds of heritage: its collections and the architecture that shelters them. Founded in nineteen twenty-nine, the institution took its present Vietnamese name in nineteen seventy-nine. Its historical collections trace Vietnam from prehistory to nineteen forty-five, while other displays explore southern Vietnam and neighbouring Asian cultures.

The older building was designed by Auguste Delaval. An octagonal central tower forms the focus of a symmetrical plan, with wings extending to either side. High ceilings and windows near the roof were intended to bring daylight into the galleries. The museum describes this architecture as a meeting of Eastern and Western influences.

Objects add personal stories to that broad view of the past. One collection bears the name of Vương Hồng Sển, a cultural researcher and antiquities collector. He assembled ceramics and other objects over his lifetime, then left his collection and books to the state.

The museum's account highlights his interest in blue-and-white ceramics. It also describes objects made in Vietnam, China, Cambodia, Japan, Thailand and France. Seen together, these pieces invite questions about craftsmanship, taste and the movement of objects between places.

Look beyond a date or the name of a dynasty. Consider the material, the maker and the person who once valued an object. Here, the building and the collections offer connected ways to explore the region's history.

## POI-008 — Bảo tàng Mỹ thuật TP.HCM

English name: **Ho Chi Minh City Fine Arts Museum**.

### Narration EN

Ho Chi Minh City Fine Arts Museum offers a way to explore Vietnamese history through images, materials and the choices of artists. The museum was established in nineteen eighty-seven. Its collection brings modern painting into conversation with older forms of sculpture and decorative art.

The buildings are part of that experience. The national tourism record describes a former residence associated with the merchant Hứa Bổn Hòa, with architectural details drawing on Asian and European traditions. Red roof tiles, patterned coloured glass and decorative floor tiles give the setting its own visual character. An arched entrance and large columns help organise the front of the main building.

The heritage authority's description divides the displays across three buildings. One focuses on modern art, including work associated with the Indochina and Gia Định art schools and the period after nineteen seventy-five. Another provides space for thematic presentations. The third introduces earlier art through objects made from stone, ceramics, bronze and wood.

As you think about those materials, notice how each allows an artist or craftsperson to work differently. A painted surface, a carved form and a ceramic object can each hold a different kind of detail. The collection offers opportunities to compare their effects.

Look at colour, texture and composition, and consider what draws your attention first. The museum places individual works within a wider story of artistic expression in Vietnam.

## POI-009 — Tòa nhà Landmark 81

English name: **Landmark 81**.

### Narration EN

Landmark eighty-one turns a familiar image from Vietnamese life into the outline of a skyscraper. Its cluster of rising forms recalls a bundle of bamboo. In the architect's design account, that image expresses strength gained by bringing individual parts together.

Atkins designed the tower for Vingroup's Central Park development in Ho Chi Minh City. The design story begins with a square plan arranged around a central core. As the building rises, its form changes, creating setbacks and a slender profile towards the top. The architects linked those changes to practical questions, including how the space needed for lifts reduces at higher levels.

Seen from outside, the different heights give the tower a rhythm of vertical lines. Try following one part of the cluster upwards, then notice where another ends. The bamboo idea becomes a way of reading the composition.

The tower has eighty-one floors and combines residential, shopping, hotel and observation spaces. That mixture connects the skyline image with different scales of everyday use, from spaces close to the ground to viewpoints high above the city.

Consider how the design moves between those scales. A skyline landmark is seen from a distance, but it also contains rooms, routes and places where people spend time. Landmark eighty-one offers an example of a modern tower whose designers used a local cultural reference to give a large urban project a recognisable identity.

## POI-010 — Tòa nhà Bitexco

English name: **Bitexco Financial Tower**.

### Narration EN

Bitexco Financial Tower gives a modern skyline form to an image drawn from nature. Designed by Carlos Zapata, the sixty-eight-storey building was completed in two thousand and ten. Its developer describes a lotus-inspired design intended to express the energy and aspirations of Vietnam.

The architect's own account develops that idea through the shape of the building. A glass curtain wall curves around the facade in forms compared with petals. The result invites you to follow a continuous surface upwards, noticing how the tower's outline changes as it rises.

One feature interrupts that movement: the projecting helipad. It extends out from one side, with more of the tower continuing above it. From a distance, this platform becomes a distinctive part of the silhouette. Its position gives the eye another line to follow across the building.

The architects also had to support that projecting platform. Their account describes steel beams extending outward from the tower's central structure. The memorable shape and the engineering needed to support it are parts of the same design problem. Looking at the platform can bring both into view.

Try looking at the building first as a whole, then through one detail: the curve of glass, the projecting platform, or the relationship between them. Consider how each contributes to the building's outline. The lotus reference connects a piece of modern construction with a symbol already familiar in Vietnamese culture.
